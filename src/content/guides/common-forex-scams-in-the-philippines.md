Philippine regulators have repeatedly flagged one pattern above all others: schemes that use the language of forex trading to run what is, functionally, an unregistered investment solicitation. Recognizing the pattern matters more than memorizing any specific company name, because scam operations rebrand constantly.

## Pattern 1: The "managed account" pitch

You're told to deposit funds and let a "professional trader" or "signal provider" manage the account for you, often with promised fixed weekly or monthly returns. Legitimate regulated brokers do not guarantee returns — trading carries genuine risk of loss. Any fixed, guaranteed-return pitch is a hallmark of the schemes the SEC has repeatedly warned about.

## Pattern 2: Recruitment-driven growth

The opportunity is presented alongside incentives for recruiting other investors, with earnings tied more to bringing in new participants than to actual trading performance. This recruitment structure is a classic warning sign regardless of the "forex" label attached to it.

## Pattern 3: Withdrawal friction

Deposits are fast and frictionless; withdrawals suddenly require additional "fees," "taxes," or "verification deposits" before funds are released. Regulated brokers do not ask you to pay to receive your own money.

## Pattern 4: Borrowed legitimacy

A scheme references a real, regulated broker's name or license number to appear credible, while the actual entity you're sending money to is unrelated. This is exactly why the license verification steps in our [broker license verification guide](/guides/how-to-verify-a-forex-broker-license/) — checking directly on the regulator's own register — matter more than trusting a name you recognize.

## Pattern 5: Social and community pressure

Many of these schemes spread through personal networks — family, church groups, local community chats — which makes the social proof feel stronger than it is. Being recommended by someone you trust doesn't substitute for verifying the entity yourself.

## What to do if you're unsure about a specific company

1. Search its name against the SEC Philippines advisory/warning list.
2. Verify any broker license number directly on the relevant regulator's own register.
3. Be skeptical of guaranteed returns, recruitment incentives, and withdrawal delays — these three patterns cover the large majority of reported cases.

## If you've already lost money

Two things are worth knowing immediately. First, the [reporting process — SEC, NBI, PNP-ACG](/guides/how-to-report-an-investment-scam-philippines/) — starts with preserving evidence, not with filing. Second, expect to be approached by someone offering to recover your funds for a fee; that is [almost always a second scam](/guides/recovery-scams-explained/).
